Two of our senior rangers have spent this month at the Southern African Wildlife College in South Africa, on a programme in wildlife movements and operations.

The college is the region's main training institution for conservation and protected-area management. The programme covers how wildlife moves across a landscape through the seasons, and how patrol, capture and translocation work is planned around that movement.

The course group at the Southern African Wildlife College.

Why we sent senior people

We sent two of our most experienced rangers rather than new recruits, because what comes back has to be taught on. It goes into how we write our own courses and how we brief the teams we put onto client land.

For a landowner or a conservancy manager, this is the difference between a patrol roster drawn on a map and one drawn around where the animals actually are. Knowing how game shifts between water, grazing and cover through the dry season tells you where the snare lines will go in before they go in.

Meanwhile, on the snare line

While they were away, the teams on the ground were working the other half of the job.

Wire snaring is the quiet end of poaching. A snare costs nothing, takes no skill to set, and does not choose its animal. It catches whatever walks into it and most of it is never collected.

An impala lost to a wire snare.

One of our rangers lifting the snare off a zebra.

Every snare lifted is one that cannot catch anything else. It is slow work, it photographs badly, and it is most of what anti-poaching actually is.

Where we are

Since 2015 we have trained more than 500 rangers. We currently have teams deployed across eight locations, covering around 50,000 hectares of Zimbabwean conservation land.
